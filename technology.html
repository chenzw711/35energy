The yield is in the degrees.

A module is rated at 25 °C. An operating module in Riyadh, Karachi or Sicily spends most of its life well above that. Heterojunction passivation keeps the voltage from collapsing as the junction heats — and that is where the extra energy actually comes from.

The mechanism

Two layers of amorphous silicon do the work.

A heterojunction cell puts a thin intrinsic amorphous silicon layer between the crystalline wafer and the doped contact. That passivates the surface — it stops electrons and holes from recombining at the boundary — and it does so at temperatures below 200 °C.

Better passivation means higher open-circuit voltage. Voc is the single largest lever on cell efficiency, and HJT's advantage there is structural rather than incremental.

Lower process temperature means less thermal damage to the wafer. A diffusion furnace runs above 800 °C; heterojunction deposition runs under 200 °C. The wafer keeps its bulk lifetime, which is what makes thin wafers viable in the first place.

Symmetrical structure means a working rear side. Both faces get the same passivation stack, so the back of the module generates — not just the front.

Carbon

Thin wafers are a carbon argument, not just a cost argument.

Silicon is the most energy-intensive input in a module. Cutting wafer thickness from 150 µm to 100 µm removes a third of that material — and the polysilicon energy embedded in it.

  • 01

    Less polysilicon per watt

    A thinner wafer means less high-purity silicon, and silicon purification is where most of a module's embodied energy sits.

  • 02

    Lower process energy

    Deposition under 200 °C instead of a diffusion furnace above 800 °C. Less electricity per cell, before you count the thinner wafer.

  • 03

    Documented, not asserted

    A carbon footprint claim is only worth what the underlying study says. We commission product-level assessments and release them with the datasheet package — we do not put "low carbon" on a banner and leave it there.
